# Phase I – Arc 1 Report: Biosensors and Foundational

**Author: Francky Duperval**\
**Project: Bio‑Aware Blended Spaces (BABS)**

## Arc 1 Overview and Purpose
Arc 1 establishes the conceptual, historical, and technical foundation of BABS. Its purpose is to
establish the project within the history of bio‑art and biosensor‑driven interactive systems, to
clarify why specific biosignals were chosen, and to articulate the constraints and affordances
that shape all subsequent phases.
This arc does not attempt full system integration. Instead, it defines what biosignals are, why
they matter artistically, how controllable they are, and what technical realities must be
understood and respected when working with them in real‑time immersive environments.
Arc 1 frames BABS as a response to a long‑standing artistic desire: creating spaces that
respond not to explicit mechanical input, but to the internal physiological state of the human
body.

## Historical Lineage of Bio‑Art
The origin point of bio‑art is commonly traced to Alvin Lucier’s 1965 work, the first musical
composition created using brainwaves. Using a medical EEG device, Lucier attempted to
intentionally generate alpha waves, which controlled servo motors attached to percussion
instruments. This work established three principles that continue to shape BABS:
1. The body can act as a generative input source
2. Control can be partial or indirect
3. Artistic meaning can emerge from involuntary physiology

As bio‑art evolved, artists increasingly explored involuntary bodily processes such as
electrodermal activity (EDA), shifting the focus away from conscious control and toward internal
state reflection.

By the 2000s, bio‑art expanded into large‑scale public installations. Works such as Pulse Room
captured the heart rates of entire audiences and expressed them spatially through arrays of
incandescent bulbs. Later projects like Pulse of the City (2012) and consumer‑facing artifacts
such as NeuroWear’s cat ears demonstrated the migration of biosensing from laboratories into
public, playful, and wearable contexts.
Arc 1 positions BABS as a continuation of this trajectory, while explicitly integrating immersive
and blended reality technologies.

## Technical Foundations of Biosensors
A biosensor is defined as an integrated receptor–transducer device capable of converting a
biological response into a measurable electrical signal.

### Core Components
**1. Analyte**\
**2. Bioreceptor**\
**3. Transducer**\
**Electronics (amplification and processing)**\
**Display or output system**

### Generations of Biosensors
- **First generation**: Mediator‑less amperometric sensors
- **Second generation**: Mediator‑based sensors with enhanced efficiency
- **Third generation**: Direct interaction between biological element and electrode
Arc 1 establishes this framework to justify sensor selection in later arcs.

## The Nature of Biosignals
Biosensor signals are inherently weak and fragile. EEG signals, in particular, exist in the
microvolt range—hundreds of times weaker than static electricity generated by everyday actions
such as standing up from a couch. Because of this, biosignals are highly susceptible to noise
and artifacts introduced by:
- Electrical hum from power sources
- Flickering lights
- Muscle movement and blinking
- Environmental electromagnetic interference

This fragility historically forced early bio‑art works to exist in highly controlled laboratory
conditions. Arc 1 acknowledges that any contemporary biosensor‑driven artistic system must
address signal amplification, filtering, and validation as core design problems rather than
afterthoughts.

## Prevalence of Biosensors in Interactive Art
Studies of Interactive Music Systems (IMSs) from 1965–2019 reveal a clear hierarchy of
biosensor usage, with EEG appearing in approximately 55.9% of surveyed systems, followed by
EMG, ECG, and EDA. This distribution has been documented in research examining how
biosensors are appropriated as embodied control structures within interactive music systems,
where EEG is often favored for its association with incorporeal control despite its instability and
ambiguity.
- EEG: ~55.9% (most prevalent)
- EMG: second most common
- ECG and EDA: follow

EEG’s dominance is tied to its symbolic and practical association with incorporeal
communication—the idea of controlling external systems using the mind alone. However, as
noted in user-centered taxonomies of biosensing in interactive art, this prevalence also exposes
EEG’s limitations: noisy data, low signal strength, and ambiguous user control.
Highlighting that prevalence does not imply ease of use or artistic clarity.

## Taxonomy of Control in Biosensor Art
A recurring challenge in biosensor‑driven systems is user controllability. Prior research in
biosensing for interactive art has proposed taxonomies that classify biosignals according to
perceived control, bodily awareness, and the type of experience they enable for participants,
framing biosignals according to their relationship with the nervous system.

### High Control Signals
- Linked to the somatic nervous system
- Example: EMG
- Highly trainable and intentional
- Often mapped to higher‑level musical or structural control

### Low Control Signals

- Linked to the sympathetic nervous system (fight‑or‑flight)
- Examples: ECG, EDA
- Reflect arousal and stress rather than intention
- Typically mapped to ambient or global transformations

### Middle Control Signals
- EEG occupies an unstable middle ground
- Contains both controllable components (e.g., blinking) and largely involuntary activity
- Offers broad creative potential but resists precise mapping

## Biosensors and Immersive Virtual Reality (IVR)
Contemporary biosensing research increasingly intersects with immersive virtual reality.
Systematic reviews of wearable biosensor usage in IVR environments indicate that most
implementations prioritize ease of measurement and robustness over expressive depth, often
limiting biosensors to background data collection rather than active, real‑time feedback.
However, most IVR systems still use biosensors primarily for data collection rather than
real‑time feedback.

Current trends include:
- Heart rate: Most common sensor in IVR (~53.3%) due to affordability and
non‑intrusiveness
- EEG: Used less frequently (~14.2%), mainly for attention and concentration tasks
- Eye tracking: Rare (~5.1%) despite hardware availability, due to data complexity

Arc 1 identifies a gap: while biosensors are common, truly adaptive, real‑time biofeedback
environments remain rare outside psychology and medicine.

## The Role of AI and Multi‑Participant Spaces
As biosensor data becomes more complex (e.g., EEG bands, eye tracking), machine learning
becomes essential for interpretation. Prior work on biosensor interfaces for interactive media art
and clinical device selection emphasizes that AI‑driven interpretation is often necessary to
translate raw biometric data into meaningful experiential changes, particularly when signals are
noisy, multidimensional, or weak. Arc 1 frames AI not as an aesthetic generator, but as an
interpreter—translating raw physiological data into meaningful environmental responses.

Emerging trends include:
- Personalized adaptive environments
- Shared multi‑participant spaces responding to collective physiological states

BABS explicitly aligns with this direction, envisioning intelligent rooms that participate with their
inhabitants.

## Generative vs Transformative Paradigms

Arc 1 distinguishes two dominant artistic strategies:

- Generative systems: Biosignals create entirely new content in real time
- Transformative systems: Biosignals alter existing media (e.g., texture, density, color)

BABS aligns primarily with the generative paradigm, while retaining the ability to incorporate
transformative layers. This distinction informs later arcs where mapping strategies become more
intentional and constrained.

## Performance Requirements and Ethical

### Considerations

High‑performance biosensing systems require:
- High selectivity
- High sensitivity
- Reproducibility
- Stability across environments

Additionally, Arc 1 emphasizes the importance of contextual analysis:
- Lab vs naturalistic settings
- Verification and validation of data
- Preprocessing and artifact correction
- Consideration of hormonal, cultural, and social factors

These considerations prevent biosignals from being treated as neutral or universal.

## 11. Arc 1 Outcome

Arc 1 concludes by establishing that biosensor‑driven art is:
- Historically grounded
- Technically constrained
- Artistically powerful precisely because of its instability

This arc provides the conceptual scaffolding for Phase 1’s subsequent arcs, where BABS
transitions from theory into constrained experimentation and proof‑of‑concept implementation.
Arc 1 does not focus on the implementation of biosensing—it defines the conditions under which
meaningful experimentation becomes possible.
